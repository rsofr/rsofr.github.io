The Friendship Paradox and Null Models in the Marvel Multiverse

September 12, 2026
7 min read
By Romeo Sofia

Have you ever felt less popular than your friends on social media? Rest assured: 87.4% of Marvel superheroes feel exactly the same way. In the pages of comic books, just like on Instagram, your friends almost systematically have more friends than you do. But why does this mathematical phenomenon occur, and how can we prove that superhero teams (Avengers, X-Men) are not merely an artifact of chance? A computational deep dive into the giant connected component of the Marvel graph.

1. Introduction: When Topology Challenges Intuition

As part of Exercise 2.11 ("Go nuts with your LLM") for the DTU 02805 course (Social Graphs and Interactions), we push the analysis of the Marvel network beyond simple descriptive metrics. By focusing on the largest connected component (LCC) of the network—comprising 277 superheroes and 1,421 undirected alliances—we will dissect two fundamental enigmas of network science:

  1. The friendship paradox: Why are your neighbors mathematically more connected than you, who are the "culprits" pulling this average up, and who manages to escape it?
  2. The null model test (Shuffle Test): Is the heroes' high clustering rate a simple byproduct of the existence of a few ultra-connected stars, or the undeniable reflection of a true community structure?

2. The Friendship Paradox Among Superheroes

Formulated for the first time by sociologist Scott L. Feld in 1991, the friendship paradox states that "your friends have on average more friends than you do." Let's apply this principle to real Marvel data.

A. The raw numbers of the network

If we randomly select a character from the giant component with uniform probability, their average degree is:

⟨k⟩ = 10.26 connections

However, if we take that same character and randomly choose one of their neighbors (or if we sample a random edge), the average degree of that neighbor skyrockets:

⟨kneighbor⟩ = 22.25 connections   |   Average of neighbors ⟨knn⟩ = 24.90

Result: A superhero's partners have on average more than twice as many allies (x2.17) as the hero themselves!

The Mathematical Foundation of the Paradox

This phenomenon is not a psychological bias, but a strict geometric property. In any heterogeneous network, the probability of reaching a node by following a random edge is proportional to its degree k. The expected degree of a neighbor is written as:

⟨kneighbor⟩ = ⟨k²⟩ / ⟨k⟩ = ⟨k⟩ + σk² / ⟨k⟩
As long as the degree variance is non-zero (σk² = 123.04 in our Marvel network), ⟨kneighbor⟩ is strictly greater than ⟨k⟩. The more super-hubs a network contains, the wider the gap!

B. Who are the Hubs responsible for the paradox?

The "culprits" are the omnipresent heroes who appear in the address books of a multitude of other characters. By being everyone's neighbors, they pull their peers' average upward.

C. Who escapes the paradox?

A character does not suffer from the paradox if their own degree is greater than or equal to the average degree of their neighbors (ki ≥ knn, i). In the Marvel network:

  • Only 35 superheroes out of 277 (12.6%) are immune to the paradox. They constitute the ultra-connected aristocracy of the universe (Spider-Man, Wolverine, Hulk, Dr. Strange, etc.).
  • 242 superheroes (87.4%) suffer the full force of the paradox: their allies systematically have more connections than they do.

3. The Shuffle Test: Does Chance Explain the Clustering?

In our first article, we observed that the Marvel network has a very high average clustering coefficient:

⟨Creal⟩ = 0.3199   (Transitivity T = 0.1823)

But a critical network science question arises: is this propensity to form triadic alliances simply a mechanical consequence of the fact that some heroes have an enormous degree? Indeed, two heroes randomly connected to Spider-Man naturally have a non-zero chance of also being connected to each other.

A. The degree-preserving Null Model protocol

To rigorously test this hypothesis, we use the Double Edge Swap algorithm with NetworkX's nx.double_edge_swap function:

  1. We select two random edges (u, v) and (x, y) with no pre-existing cross-link.
  2. We swap them to form (u, y) and (x, v).
  3. We repeat the operation 14,210 times per network (i.e., 10 times the total number of edges) to destroy any local correlation while preserving 100% of the exact degree of each character.
  4. We generate 100 independent replicates of this null model.

B. Statistical results and Z-score

The distribution of the clustering coefficients measured on these 100 synthetic networks yields a spectacular result:

  • Null model average: ⟨Cnull⟩ = 0.1554 ± 0.0084 (extremes: [0.1399 ; 0.1816])
  • Real clustering: ⟨Creal⟩ = 0.3199
  • Clustering Z-score: +19.57 σ
  • Empirical p-value: p = 0.0000 (p < 10-3)

Interpretation: Why isn't chance enough?

This test irrefutably proves the massive rejection of the null hypothesis. Granted, having super-hubs increases clustering from 0.04 (in an Erdős-Rényi graph without hubs) to 0.15. But that only explains half of the observed cohesion!

The remaining gap (+19.6 σ) is the direct mathematical footprint of social and narrative triadic closure: in Marvel, heroes organize into tight-knit teams (Avengers, Fantastic Four, X-Men, Defenders). My ally's ally is very often my ally because we share the same faction, the same base of operations, or the same editorial arc.

4. Conclusion & What Network Science Teaches Us

This computational exploration in Exercise 2.11 demonstrates the inexhaustible richness of comic book graphs:

  • The friendship paradox spares no one: Less than 13% of heroes (the major super-hubs like Spider-Man or Wolverine) are more popular than their peers. The remaining 87% revolve around allies who are much more connected than they are.
  • The community structure is real: The Marvel universe's exceptional clustering is not an artifact caused by the popularity of a few icons. It is the result of a dense narrative modularity forged by 80 years of collective alliances.

By combining mathematical rigor, null models, and custom visualizations, network science transforms a fictional universe into a fascinating laboratory for understanding the relational architectures of the real world.
