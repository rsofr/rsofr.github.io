Topology of the Marvel Network: Who is the true center of gravity in the comic universe?

September 2, 2026
8 min read
By Romeo Sofia

With over eight decades of continuous publication, more than 19,000 documented characters, and nearly 100,000 narrative collaborations, the Marvel Comics universe is not just popular entertainment: it is a fascinating subject of study in network science. By modeling the co-occurrences of superheroes within the same issue, we uncover a topology with rigorous mathematical properties.

1. From Multiverse to Graph: Mathematical Modeling

To transform this narrative universe into an object of computational analysis, we begin by formalizing a bipartite (bimodal) graph denoted G = (P, C, E), where:

  • P represents the discrete set of characters (heroes, villains, cosmic entities).
  • C represents the set of comics (issues and publications).
  • E connects a character p ∈ P to a comic c ∈ C if the character explicitly appears in the issue.

To study direct relational affinities, we perform a unimodal projection onto the character space. In this new graph, two characters are connected by a weighted edge corresponding to the total number of shared editions.

Definition: Scale-Free Network

As demonstrated by Barabási and Albert, the degree distribution P(k) of this network follows a power law: P(k) ∼ k-γ. The vast majority of heroes have very few connections, while a handful of super-hubs concentrate the bulk of the interactions.

2. Centrality Analysis: The True Pillars

In network science, being "important" does not have a single definition. Two fundamental metrics reveal two distinct ways to dominate the Marvel universe:

A. Degree Centrality: Brute omnipresence

This measures the number of immediate neighbors a node has. In this game, Captain America (Steve Rogers) sits on the throne. As a founding member and institutional linchpin of the Avengers, he has fought alongside more than 1,900 different heroes.

B. Betweenness Centrality: The universe crosser

Betweenness CB(v) quantifies the number of shortest paths (geodesics) passing through node v:

CB(v) = ∑s ≠ v ≠ t [ σst(v) / σst ]

And this is where the most spectacular result occurs: Spider-Man (Peter Parker) surpasses all other superheroes. Why? Because Peter Parker is a "street-level hero" who collaborates just as easily with the Avengers as he does with the X-Men, the Fantastic Four, or urban heroes like Daredevil. He acts as the critical bridge that prevents the graph from splitting into isolated connected components.

3. The Small-World Phenomenon

Much like human social networks studied by Watts and Strogatz, the Marvel graph exhibits a spectacular Small-World property:

  • A high clustering coefficient: A superhero's partners have a very high probability of having already fought together within a team.
  • A very short average distance: The average geodesic distance between any two arbitrary characters in the Marvel universe is less than 3.1 hops.
The lab anecdote: The "Kevin Bacon" of comics

Did you know that a character as obscure as Howard the Duck is only 2 edges away from Magneto and 3 edges away from Galactus? Thanks to the pivotal status of Spider-Man and Wolverine, global connectivity remains highly resilient.

4. Conclusion & Research Perspectives

This foray into comic book topology demonstrates the power of graph theory tools to model massive complex systems. Our next work will focus on the temporal evolution of the graph through major editorial events (Civil War, Secret Wars) and the algorithmic detection of communities via the Louvain algorithm.
